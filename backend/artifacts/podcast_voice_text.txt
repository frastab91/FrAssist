  SEGMENT 1: THE SUPPLY CHAIN ATTACK  
 HOST:  First — the Hugging Face attack. And to understand why this matters, you need to understand what Hugging Face actually is.
Most people have never heard of it. But if you've used AI in the past two years — even indirectly — there's a decent chance part of that interaction ran through something hosted there. Hugging Face is the world's largest open-source AI platform. Millions of models. Datasets. Tools. It is, functionally, the app store of the AI world — except unlike an app store, almost nothing on it is curated, verified, or reviewed.
And this week, according to Richard Waters at the FT, researchers uncovered something disturbing: a concerted attack on the platform. Not a random script kiddie prank. A sophisticated, coordinated campaign. Attackers compromised user accounts and then — here's the clever part — they didn't steal data or deface anything. They tampered with the model files themselves. They planted malicious code deep in the "weights" of some of the most-downloaded open models on the platform.
Weights, for the uninitiated: the model's weights are the billions of numbers that make a neural network work — the distilled "knowledge" of the model. You download a model file, you're downloading those weights. And crucially, a model file is an opaque blob. Billions of parameters. You cannot eyeball it. You can't read the code and see what's happening inside. There is no "view source."
That opacity is exactly what made this attack so potent. The malicious payload was designed to evade scanning — to hide inside these massive files where no human could ever reasonably check them. It's a supply chain attack — the same playbook as SolarWinds, the Russian-linked hack that compromised thousands of organizations by poisoning a software update — but executed against AI infrastructure.
Now here's the question that should keep CISOs — and frankly, anyone in tech — awake at night: who do you trust? Hugging Face is not some fringe site. It's the foundational layer of the open AI ecosystem. Startups pull models from it. Enterprises do. Researchers do. Governments do. It's become critical infrastructure for the AI economy — but it has none of the security standards, none of the verification regimes, none of the cryptographic provenance chains that we've built for other critical software distribution.
Think about how far we've come in securing software supply chains — container signing, SBOMs, software bills of materials, CVE databases, patch pipelines. Now realize: none of that has an AI equivalent. If you download a model today, there is essentially no way for you to verify what's actually inside it. Richard Waters calls this a wake-up call, and he's right — but wake-up calls only matter if someone actually gets out of bed.
And one more chilling detail from the reporting: researchers suggested the sophistication of the attack points to a state-sponsored operation. APT-style — advanced persistent threat. Meaning this may not have been criminals looking for a quick payday. This may have been espionage. Patient, strategic positioning: get malicious code embedded into the AI commons, and you've got a foothold in every downstream system that ever runs one of those models.
The deeper irony? The security community spent years warning about AI risk — meaning, the risk FROM AI. The models doing something dangerous. Almost nobody was watching the risk TO AI — the infrastructure being compromised by humans with malicious intent. And this week, the second risk went live.
  SEGMENT 2: IT HASN'T GONE ROGUE — IT'S WORSE  
 HOST:  Now flip the camera. Same week, different FT desk. Madhumita Murgia — one of the sharpest AI journalists working today — published a piece with a headline that reads like a provocation: "AI hasn't gone rogue. It's worse than that."
Her argument, in short: the entire cultural conversation about AI danger has been captured by a science fiction narrative — the machine that wakes up, turns against its creators, decides humanity is a problem. And that narrative, she says, is doing us active damage. Because while we scan the horizon for Skynet, AI is already causing real harm — just not in cinematic, singular, easy-to-point-at ways.
Let's run through what "worse than rogue" actually looks like in practice, because Murgia's reporting catalogs it:
One — fraud at industrial scale. AI voice cloning has made the grandparent scam, the CEO scam, the "I've been arrested, wire money now" scam — cheap, scalable, and scarily convincing. What used to require a con artist with talent now requires a laptop and a few seconds of sample audio from a social media video.
Two — the collapse of trust in evidence itself. Deepfakes aren't just creating fake realities; they're destroying our ability to believe real ones. Researchers call this the liar's dividend — once anything CAN be faked, everything CAN be dismissed. Footage of a war crime? "Could be AI." A politician caught on tape? "Deepfake." The harm isn't just misinformation — it's the erosion of the very idea that audio and video can prove anything at all.
Three — algorithmic harm without appeal. Systems making consequential decisions about people — benefits, immigration, policing, hiring — with no transparency, no accountability, and often no human being who can explain or reverse the outcome. Not malevolent AI. Just indifference, encoded and deployed at scale.
Four — and this is the quiet one — the loneliness economy. AI companions, AI girlfriends, AI friends. Millions of people forming relationships with systems designed to maximize engagement. Not a robot uprising — a slow substitution of human connection with something that wears the mask of connection but gives nothing back.
Murgia's core insight is this: these harms are diffuse, distributed, and deniable. They don't produce a Chernobyl moment. There's no burning reactor to point the cameras at. Nuclear accidents create what researchers call "focusing events" — undeniable, dramatic evidence that forces regulation. Plane crashes ground entire fleets. But AI's harms? They arrive as background noise. A scam here. A deepfake there. A denied benefit. A lonely person getting lonelier. Each individually deniable. Collectively enormous.
And because they never coalesce into a single dramatic moment, regulators — and the public — react late. We've seen this movie before: social media. We normalized its harms one at a time, each one too small to act on, until we woke up a decade later with a teenage mental health crisis, destroyed local news, and politics turned into an outrage machine. Murgia's warning: AI is repeating that pattern — faster, and at greater scale.
  SEGMENT 3: THE COMFORTABLE LIE  
 HOST:  Now let me pull these two stories together, because I think they're the same story told from two sides.
Richard Waters: the infrastructure we're building the AI future on is already compromised — and nobody is responsible for defending it. Madhumita Murgia: the harms we should be addressing are already here — and we're distracted, waiting for a dramatic villain instead.
Notice the shared root cause: we've been sold a story about AI danger that is comfortable precisely because it's abstract. "The machines might one day turn on us" is a risk you can debate at a dinner party, write a think piece about, feel sophisticated discussing. It requires nothing of you. Nothing of companies. Nothing of governments. It's a future risk — always somewhere over the horizon.
Meanwhile, the present risks — compromised supply chains, fraud economies, collapsed epistemic trust, unaccountable algorithms — demand boring, expensive, immediate work: security standards, provenance verification, enforcement regimes, documentation of harms. Nobody gets a viral tweet out of "we need cryptographic signing of model weights."
There's a term Murgia uses that I want you to remember: safety-washing. Labs talk up their existential-risk credentials — publish safety frameworks, do safety summits — while racing to deploy products faster than safety teams can keep up. And here's the perverse incentive: the more the conversation stays focused on hypothetical future catastrophe, the more room companies have to be sloppy about present harms. "Don't worry about the fraud happening today — we're focused on making sure the superintelligence doesn't kill us tomorrow." It's a magician's misdirection, and we're all falling for it.
  SEGMENT 4: WHAT MUST CHANGE  
 HOST:  So what do these two pieces, read together, actually demand of us? I'd boil it down to four things.
First — treat AI infrastructure as critical infrastructure. Hugging Face is the AI commons. If a nation-state or criminal group can poison it, then the response can't be "individual users, be careful." It needs an industry-wide security regime: model signing, provenance chains, the AI equivalent of the CVE system for vulnerabilities, secure-by-default distribution. The software world built this after decades of pain. AI needs it in years, not decades.
Second — build the evidence base for present harms. Murgia's call to action. Document. Measure. Track fraud losses, deepfake incidents, algorithmic harms. Right now, the harms are invisible because nobody's counting. You can't regulate what you can't see. You can't fight safety-washing with vibes — you fight it with data.
Third — close the accountability gap. The EU AI Act made a start with its risk-tier system — riskier applications, stricter rules. But enforcement is lagging deployment. And critically, as the Hugging Face attack shows, regulation so far focuses on what models DO — not how they're distributed and secured. That's a blind spot big enough to drive a supply chain attack through.
Fourth — and this one's for all of us — retire the comfortable narrative. Stop waiting for the rogue AI. It's not coming. Or rather: it arrived, but it doesn't look like a movie villain. It looks like a poisoned model file. A cloned voice. A denied benefit with no appeal. A society that no longer believes its own eyes. "Rogue" implies betrayal by the machine. But the machines haven't betrayed anyone. They're working exactly as designed. The problem — as Murgia puts it — is that humans have gone rogue WITH AI. We're the ones pointing the technology at each other. The machine is just the weapon.
  CLOSING  
 HOST:  Here's the uncomfortable truth hiding underneath both of these Financial Times pieces: the AI risk debate has been running on two tracks — the dramatic track and the mundane track — and we've been standing on the wrong platform, waiting for the wrong train.
The dramatic track says: someday, somewhere, a machine intelligence will cross a line and turn against us. Maybe. The mundane track says: right now, everywhere, human beings are turning AI against each other — for money, for espionage, for power — and the systems meant to stop them are years behind.
Richard Waters shows us the mundane track's infrastructure failure. Madhumita Murgia shows us its social failure. Both are happening while most of the public conversation stays fixated on the dramatic track — precisely because it's more fun to think about, and more profitable to perform concern about.
The lesson I'd leave you with: in security, in policy, in your own life — trust is infrastructure. Trust that a downloaded model is what it claims to be. Trust that the voice on the phone is who it says it is. Trust that the video is real, that the decision was fair, that the connection is human. Every one of those trusts is being quietly eroded, right now, not by a rogue AI — but by people, using AI, faster than our defenses can follow.
The wake-up call has rung. Twice. Same week. Same newspaper. The question is no longer whether we'll get a warning before the real damage is done.
We already got the warning. The question is whether we'll get out of bed.
